## Clusters (named as patterns)

### Pattern 1: Safety slips in the days between the specialist's visits, because nobody is checking

**Interviews:** 1, 2, 3, 4, 7 (5 of 7)

**Insights in this cluster:**
1. The specialist comes once every 4 days and tells workers what not to violate; nothing is checked in between. (Interview 2)
2. The company paid about 150,000 GEL in fines over 4 months because workers repeated the same violations and the specialist was not always on site. (Interview 2)
3. The specialist leaves a checklist, but nobody makes sure it is followed between visits. (Interview 2)
4. The specialist says he cannot be on site 24 hours a day or watch for simple violations at every moment. (Interview 3)
5. "No, in our case, they simply point out any violations verbally and leave. Until the next visit, monitoring that issue relies solely on my verbal oversight." (Interview 1)
6. "As the founder, I have no idea whether workers on-site are actually wearing helmets and harnesses today, until the Labor Inspection arrives." (Interview 4)
7. "There's no paperwork or checklist we fill out at the end of the day." (Interview 7)
8. "As an engineer, my primary focus is technical execution; I can't chase every worker every minute to keep their helmets on." (Interview 1)
9. "The inspection fine isn't just 2,000 GEL, if a site is halted for 3 days due to a safety violation, the delay penalty from the developer costs me up to 5,000 GEL per day." (Interview 4)


**What it shows:** The owner and the specialist describe the same gap from both sides: rules and guidance exist, but no one enforces them between visits. The engineer, the founder and a worker confirm it from their own positions, and the owner only finds out when the inspector does.

---

### Pattern 2: Protection is available on site but gets undone during normal work

**Interviews:** 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 7 (6 of 7)


**Insights in this cluster:**
1. PPE is almost always available; workers don't wear all of it because it is uncomfortable. (Interview 2)
2. Missing edge fences are the most common violation: they are removed while lifting materials and not put back. (Interview 3)
3. The team removed an edge fence to lift something and nobody put it back. (Interview 5)
4. "We had the equipment fully available on-site; it's simply a matter of habit. It's hot, the helmets feel tight, the footwear is uncomfortable, or sometimes they just can't be bothered to wear the gear." (Interview 1)
5. "all of us really know like basic precautions to put on our helmets" (Interview 6)
6. "We have helmets lying around, but we only put them on when we actually feel like it or if someone is watching." (Interview 7)
7. "we control even each other. It is very encouraged to urge each other to put on our helmets" (Interview 6)


**What it shows:** The equipment is not missing. It is taken off or left off while the work moves on, and nobody notices afterwards.

---

### Pattern 3: Site-order violations like litter count too, not only PPE and fences

**Interviews:** 2, 5 (2 of 7)

**Insights in this cluster:**
1. The latest labor inspection gave a warning for litter around the site, while workers were wearing full PPE. (Interview 2)
2. The team lead names litter left around the site as a common violation. (Interview 5)

**What it shows:** Inspectors look at the whole site. A team can get PPE right and still be warned for site order.

---

### Pattern 4: Safety is performed for the inspector, not kept up day to day

**Interviews:** 4, 7 (2 of 7)

**Quotes in this cluster:**
1. "We pay monthly bonuses to foremen and engineers, but filling out the safety log is still done by hand at the last minute right before an inspector arrives." (Interview 4)
2. "So we all rushed and grabbed our helmets super quick to put them on, just so we wouldn't get fined." (Interview 7)
3. "we usually have cameras around where we work, so we can see when someone arrives and we're ready for them." (Interview 7)

**What it shows:** From the founder's office to the crew, the inspection is the only moment safety is made visible: the log is written for it and helmets go on for it. The inspector sees a site that has been prepared, not the site as it runs.

---

### Pattern 5: The owner pays for violations he cannot see, and the people who see them never see what they cost

**Interviews:** 1, 4, 7 (3 of 7)

**Quotes in this cluster:**
1. "To be honest, financial matters do not fall within my scope of responsibility..." (Interview 1)
2. "As the founder, I have no idea whether workers on-site are actually wearing helmets and harnesses today, until the Labor Inspection arrives." (Interview 4)
3. "If a fine ever did happen, maybe the main office or the owner would deal with it, but we never see that side." (Interview 7)

**What it shows:** Cost and visibility sit with different people. The founder pays but is not on site (he spends 4 hours a week phoning engineers, Interview 4); the engineer and the crew are on site, but the fines never reach them.

## Outliers

1. **Work that doesn't match the architectural drawings** (Interview 5)
   - Why it's unique: it is a design-compliance problem, not a site-safety one, and needs checking against plans rather than rules.
   - Decision: set aside for now; note it and ask about it in the next interviews.

2. **The violation was found by the inspector, not by the team** (Interview 2)
   - Why it's unique: only one interview says who found the violation, so it can't be a pattern yet.
   - Decision: worth exploring; Script v2 asks "How did you find out?" for this reason.

3. **A violation treated as the worker's fault, not a company fine** (Interview 6)
   - "we were not fined because it was an individual error and not some kind of one that causes all the companies" (Interview 6)
   - Why it's unique: it cuts against Interviews 2 and 4, where violations become company fines.
   - Decision: open question in `problem-statement.md`; ask the next owner when a violation becomes a company fine.

4. **The inspector's grace period** (Interview 1)
   - The inspector set a deadline to fix the missing safety footwear and came back to check (Interview 1).
   - Decision: only one log describes it; keep watching.

```

        ↓  nobody notices because

[Pattern 5: the owner pays but cannot see; the site sees but does not pay]
        ↓  so safety is staged for the one moment it is visible

        ↓  and the inspector still finds what was missed
[Pattern 3 + outlier 2: warnings and fines, including for site order]
```

Pattern 2 is how violations start; Pattern 1 is why they stay unfixed; Patterns 5 and 4 explain why the people involved don't change it.

## Surprises and hypothesis check

**What surprised us:**
- The most common violation is edge fences, not PPE (Interviews 3, 5).
- Violations come from forgetting during work, not from refusing the rules (Interview 5).
- A fine costs far more than its face value: a 3-day stoppage brings delay penalties of up to 5,000 GEL a day (Interview 4).
- Compliance is staged: a crew member watches the gate and the crew uses its cameras to put helmets on before the inspector arrives (Interview 7).
- The site engineer sees money as outside his job: "financial matters do not fall within my scope of responsibility" (Interview 1).
- One violation was treated as an individual error and the company was not fined (Interview 6).

**What validated our hypothesis:**
- Compliance slips without oversight (Interviews 1, 2, 3, 4, 7), and fines are a large, real cost (Interviews 2, 4).

**What contradicted our hypothesis:**
- We assumed PPE was missing on site; it is available (Interviews 1, 2, 7).
- We assumed workers lacked guidance; the specialist leaves a checklist (Interview 2), though another gives only verbal feedback (Interview 1).
