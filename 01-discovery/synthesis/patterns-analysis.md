**Source:** [affinity-map.md](affinity-map.md)

Each pattern is a sentence about what the evidence says, cited by interview number. Only statements made by the interviewees are used; answers marked *(assumption)* or *(from Interview 2)* in the logs are not counted.

## Pattern 1: Safety slips in the days between the specialist's visits, because nobody is checking

**Interviews:** 1, 2, 3, 4, 7 (5 of 7)

**Evidence:**
- Interview 2: the specialist comes once every 4 days; nothing is checked in between, and his checklist is not followed up.
- Interview 2: about 150,000 GEL in fines over 4 months, from repeated violations while the specialist wasn't on site.
- Interview 3: the specialist says he cannot be on site 24 hours a day or watch for simple violations every moment.
- Interview 1: the specialist only gives verbal feedback: "Until the next visit, monitoring that issue relies solely on my verbal oversight."
- Interview 4: the founder: "I have no idea whether workers on-site are actually wearing helmets and harnesses today, until the Labor Inspection arrives."
- Interview 7: a small crew with no specialist: "There's no paperwork or checklist we fill out at the end of the day."

**Intensity:** High. It is the most expensive pattern (150,000 GEL in 4 months, Interview 2), and the owner and the specialist both describe it. The founder adds the cost of stoppages: a 2,000 GEL fine that halts a site for 3 days brings delay penalties of up to 5,000 GEL a day, more than 15,000 GEL last year, plus 4 hours a week of phone calls to site engineers (Interview 4).

**Root cause:** Oversight is periodic, but violations happen continuously. Nobody between the owner and the workers is responsible for checking safety every day.

## Pattern 2: Protection is available on site but gets undone during normal work

**Interviews:** 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 7 (6 of 7)

**Evidence:**
- Interview 2: PPE is almost always on site; workers don't wear all of it because it is uncomfortable.
- Interview 3: missing edge fences are the most common violation; they are removed while lifting materials and not put back.
- Interview 5: the team removed a fence to lift materials and nobody put it back.
- Interview 1: "We had the equipment fully available on-site; it's simply a matter of habit. It's hot, the helmets feel tight..."
- Interview 6: "It is very encouraged to urge each other to put on our helmets."
- Interview 7: "We have helmets lying around, but we only put them on when we actually feel like it or if someone is watching."

**Intensity:** High. The safety specialist calls it the most common violation, the team lead describes it happening on his own team, and it appears in 6 of 7 logs.

**Root cause:** Safety equipment gets in the way of real work (lifting, moving, comfort), so it comes off. Restoring it is nobody's specific job, so it stays off.

---

## Pattern 3: Site-order violations like litter count too, not only PPE and fences

**Interviews:** 2, 5 (2 of 7)

**Evidence:**
- Interview 2: the latest inspection gave a warning for litter around the site, while workers were wearing full PPE.
- Interview 5: the team lead names litter around the site as a common violation.

**Intensity:** Medium. The litter incident was a warning, not a fine, but it shows inspectors check the whole site.

**Root cause:** Safety is usually thought of as PPE, so site order gets less attention, even though inspectors check it.

---

## Pattern 4: Safety is performed for the inspector, not kept up day to day

**Interviews:** 4, 7 (2 of 7)

**Evidence:**
- Interview 4: "filling out the safety log is still done by hand at the last minute right before an inspector arrives."
- Interview 7: "So we all rushed and grabbed our helmets super quick to put them on, just so we wouldn't get fined."
- Interview 7: "we usually have cameras around where we work, so we can see when someone arrives and we're ready for them."

1. Why does this happen? Because the inspection is the only moment anyone outside the site sees its safety (Interview 4: the founder has "no idea" until the inspector arrives).
2. Why that? Because between inspections nothing is written down: no log is kept day to day (Interview 4) and there is no checklist at the end of the day (Interview 7).
3. Why that? Because the only consequence anyone feels is the fine at inspection, so effort goes into looking compliant on that day (Interviews 4, 7).

**Root cause:** Safety is only checked at the inspection, so people prepare for the inspection instead of keeping the site safe every day.

**Which answer is a guess:** Why 3 is our reading of two logs; ask an owner directly whether the safety log is ever filled in on a normal day.

---

## Pattern 5: The owner pays for violations he cannot see, and the people who see them never see what they cost

**Interviews:** 1, 4, 7 (3 of 7)

**Evidence:**
- Interview 1: "financial matters do not fall within my scope of responsibility..."
- Interview 4: "I have no idea whether workers on-site are actually wearing helmets and harnesses today, until the Labor Inspection arrives."; he spends 4 hours a week phoning site engineers.
- Interview 7: "If a fine ever did happen, maybe the main office or the owner would deal with it, but we never see that side."

1. Why don't people on site act on violations? Because the cost never reaches them (Interviews 1, 7).
2. Why doesn't the owner act? Because he cannot see the violations, only phone about them (Interview 4).

**Intensity:** Medium. It explains why Patterns 1 and 4 persist even though the owner loses real money.

**Root cause:** The person who pays is not on site, and the people on site do not pay.

**Which answer is a guess:** In Interview 6 the worker did bear the consequence; ask when a violation becomes the company's cost and when it becomes the worker's.

## Ranking

| Rank | Pattern | Interviews | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Safety slips between the specialist's visits | 1, 2, 3, 4, 7 | Largest cost (Interviews 2, 4); described by the engineer, owner, specialist, founder and a worker |
| 2 | Protection is undone during normal work | 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 7 | Widest evidence; explains how violations start |
| 3 | Safety is performed for the inspector | 4, 7 | Most specific finding; told from the founder's office and from the crew |
| 4 | The owner pays but cannot see | 1, 4, 7 | Explains why nobody on site changes the situation |
| 5 | Site-order violations count too | 2, 5 | Real, but lower cost so far |

**Strongest pattern:** Pattern 1. It appears in 5 of 7 logs (Interviews 1, 2, 3, 4, 7), and both cost figures we have, 150,000 GEL in fines (Interview 2) and up to 5,000 GEL a day in delay penalties (Interview 4), come from violations that went unchecked between visits.

---

## How the patterns connect

Protection gets removed during work (Pattern 2) → nobody checks between visits (Pattern 1) → the owner who pays can't see it and the site doesn't pay for it (Pattern 5) → safety is staged for the inspector (Pattern 4) → an inspector still finds what was missed, including site-order issues (Pattern 3) → warnings, fines and delay penalties.

## Draft problem statement

Mid-sized construction companies in Georgia pay large fines and daily delay penalties for simple, repeated safety violations, such as edge fences removed during lifting and not put back, PPE not worn, and litter, because their safety specialist visits only every few days, nobody checks the site in between, and safety is only made visible when an inspector is expected. (Interviews 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 7)

## Gaps

- Only Interviews 2 and 4 give cost figures, and neither breaks fines down by violation type.
- Interviews 2 and 3 come from the same company; the other five come from different sites and companies.
